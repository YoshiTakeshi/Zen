# Zen measures metal money without a custodian

Zen's defensible novelty is narrow but real. A limited search (about 100 tool calls across five research threads) found **no live or proposed cryptocurrency whose consensus rules set a unit's value against a physical asset from the chain's own payments and merchant prices**. The closest neighbours are off-consensus price estimators (UTXOracle, brk_oracle), an academic consensus design that uses transaction history to steer issuance rather than a peg (Komi & Tatekawa, ePrint 2023/373), and oracle-fed rebasing tokens (Ampleforth). The problem Zen can name in the way Bitcoin named double-spending is **the oracle problem for a commodity-denominated unit of account**: a stable, metal-referenced unit with no external price feed, no custodian and no issuer. The peer-reviewed literature still calls that problem open ([Klages-Mundt et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.12388v2)). The designer's macroeconomic thesis is a different matter. Zen claims its three metals and three layers can temper inflation and deflation cycles with nobody in control, and the historical record does not support that as stated. Every commodity system that stabilized prices relied on an identifiable agent: a mint posting buy and sell prices, an official index, note-issuing banks with capital, or a buffer-stock authority. Where metal ratios or supplies were simply left alone, Gresham's law, small-change shortages and multi-decade deflations followed. The spec should therefore make strong, testable claims about **measurement** (manipulation cost, breakdown point, rate limits, no reporters to bribe). It should make modest, conditional claims about **stability**, and it must say precisely where the physical-metal anchor enters the measurement and whether S only describes the coin's value or also drives issuance.

## No prior system reads a metal peg from its own payments in consensus

The prior art falls into four families, and each moves trust to a different place. Zen's position is clearest when set against all four.

**Custodial metal tokens peg by redemption.** e-gold (1996) is the closest ancestor in units. Its payments ("spends") were denominated in **grams** of gold, silver and other metals. It reached **5 million accounts by 2009** and over **US$2 billion a year in spends in 2006**, backed by roughly 3.8 t of gold ([Wikipedia: E-gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-gold)). The peg did not fail. The operator was prosecuted: a 2007 indictment for money laundering and unlicensed money transmission ended in a 2008 plea ([DOJ, July 2008](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2008/July/08-crm-635.html)), and a judge rejected e-gold's argument that it was not a money transmitter ([OffshoreAlert](https://offshorealert.com/us-judge-rejects-e-golds-argument-that-it-is-not-a-money-transmitter)). Today's products follow the same custody model. PAX Gold and Tether Gold each represent one troy ounce held in vaults, and Kinesis KAU represents one gram of allocated gold ([Stablecoin Insider](https://stablecoininsider.org/gold-backed-stablecoins/)). In all of them the peg is only as good as the custodian, the auditor and the regulator. Zen differs in kind. Its electronic coin is **not a claim on anyone's metal**; S is a measured exchange rate, not a liability. The honest framing is "metal as unit of account, not as backing."

**Synthetic metal tokens replace the custodian with collateral and a price feed.** BitShares' bitUSD was collateralized by BTS and triggered "global settlement" on 25 Nov 2018 after becoming under-collateralized. It stayed off its peg for more than two months ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/markets/2019-02-15-bitusd-hasnt-had-its-1-peg-for-more-than-two-months-11876)). Synthetix moved sXAU and sXAG onto Chainlink feeds in December 2019, calling its previous oracles "a potential point of failure" and warning of weekend black-swan risk while FX markets are closed ([Synthetix blog](https://blog.synthetix.io/chainlink-decentralizes-first-wave-of-synthetix-price-feeds/)). Here trust moves to whoever publishes the feed. Zen has no collateral, so it has no liquidation or global-settlement failure mode. Its failure mode is mismeasurement or drift.

**Algorithmic and rebasing designs steer toward a target using an external oracle.** Ampleforth rebases every balance daily toward a CPI-adjusted dollar, using a Chainlink VWAP feed and a monthly CPI feed ([Chainlink case study](https://chain.link/case-studies/ampleforth)). Mechanically, Zen's adjustment of S is closest to a rebase, since it changes grams per coin rather than coin supply. But Zen's input is internal payment data, not an oracle. The academic paper nearest in spirit is Komi & Tatekawa's consensus algorithm. It estimates money supply from historical transaction values and PoW difficulty and adjusts issuance and fee burning to damp exchange-rate swings, but **it derives no peg or price level from transaction amounts** ([ePrint 2023/373](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/373.pdf)).

**Endogenous on-chain price estimators are Zen's true intellectual ancestors.** UTXOracle infers BTC/USD from how round-dollar output amounts ($20, $100, $1,000) cluster on a log scale. Such outputs make up as much as **about 25% of outputs on some days**. UTXOracle reports a median daily deviation from exchange VWAP of **0.65%** over Jul 2020 to Jan 2023, with accuracy decaying after about two years and deviations above 10% in extreme volatility ([Nasdaq / Bitcoin Magazine](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/utxoracle:-a-decentralized-approach-to-the-oracle-problem)). brk_oracle refines the pipeline into a 2,400-bin log histogram with a 19-point round-dollar stencil, and self-reports a **0.15% median per-block error** over 596,251 blocks. That figure has not been independently verified ([docs.rs brk_oracle](https://docs.rs/crate/brk_oracle/0.3.4)). The UTXOracle authors name three threats, and each maps onto Zen: fake round-amount outputs (costly but visible), the impossibility of removing real transactions, and a **feedback loop once people price by the oracle** ([Bitcoin Magazine](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/technical/utxoracle-model-could-bring-use-cases-to-bitcoin)).

Against that background, Zen generalizes UTXOracle in three ways. It makes the reference grid explicit by protocol: shops price in exact sixths of a coin instead of relying on an emergent habit of round fiat amounts. It moves the estimator into consensus instead of leaving it as a side calculation. And it adds Sybil and wash defences that UTXOracle lacks: each gram counted once per rest period, coin-days and value weighting, receiver caps and bonds. The three-chain batch-auction cross-check of gold, silver and copper weights has no counterpart in the sources found. The coarse sixths grid also echoes a mitigation Buterin proposed against "micro-cheating" in SchellingCoin: coarse rounding ([Ethereum blog, 2014](https://blog.ethereum.org/2014/03/28/schellingcoin-a-minimal-trust-universal-data-feed)).

There is one objection the spec cannot dodge. If shops price in coin units and S is calibrated so that coin-priced prices match payment values, the measurement can become self-referential. That is exactly the feedback loop UTXOracle's authors admit. An external anchor to physical metal value has to enter somewhere: the shop's willingness to accept a physical coin at its posted fraction, a metal-market price, or the cross-chain exchange ratios. The spec must say which, and what happens when that anchor is weak.

> **Suggested spec text: Prior art and novelty.** Zen belongs to a family of designs that tie a digital unit to physical metal. Unlike custodial tokens (e-gold, PAX Gold, Tether Gold, Kinesis), Zen's electronic coin is not a claim on stored metal, and no custodian, auditor or redemption is involved. Unlike synthetic tokens (BitShares bitAssets, Synthetix sXAU), Zen uses no collateral and no external price feed. Unlike rebasing tokens (Ampleforth), Zen's adjustment input is the chain's own payment data rather than an oracle. Zen's measurement builds directly on endogenous on-chain price estimators, notably UTXOracle and brk_oracle, which infer an exchange rate from the clustering of round-valued payments. To our knowledge, Zen is the first design to (a) perform such an estimate inside consensus, (b) make the reference grid explicit through coin-unit pricing in exact sixths, (c) harden the estimate with per-gram, once-per-rest-period counting, coin-days weighting and receiver bonds, and (d) cross-check three metal-referenced chains through an on-chain batch auction. **Caveats:** this novelty claim rests on a limited literature and patent search and should be read as "no prior design found." The physical-metal anchor enters the measurement at [specify exactly]. Like any endogenous estimator, Zen's estimate can drift or become self-referential when most prices are set by reference to S itself; section [X] describes the anchor and band that bound this.

## Zen's "double-spend" is the oracle problem for a metal unit

Nakamoto named one problem, double-spending without a trusted third party, and proved one property: an attacker's chance of catching up falls exponentially ([Bitcoin whitepaper](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf)). The whitepaper has no mechanism for value stability. The supply schedule is fixed, and the economics literature concluded early that Bitcoin fails as a unit of account. Its volatility is "greatly higher" than that of major currencies, prices need many leading-zero decimals, and almost no credit is denominated in it ([Yermack, NBER w19747](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19747)). A credible Zen abstract should likewise name **one** primary problem and attach **one** quantitative security bound.

The best candidate is the blockchain oracle problem: a chain cannot verify an off-chain fact such as a price without bringing back a trusted party or an attackable game ([Caldarelli 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/11/11/509/htm); [Caldarelli & Ellul 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/app11167572)). For non-custodial stablecoins, "the correctness of the imported data is not objectively verifiable on-chain," decentralized methods "typically collapse in the face of game-theoretic attacks," and solving this in a fully decentralized way "remains an open problem" ([Klages-Mundt et al., AFT '20](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.12388v2)). Zen's answer is structurally different from a voting oracle. No reporter submits a value, so there is no reporter to bribe, and the P+epsilon attacks on SchellingCoin-style medians largely do not apply ([Ethereum blog](https://blog.ethereum.org/2014/03/28/schellingcoin-a-minimal-trust-universal-data-feed); [ethresear.ch on bribery bidding wars](https://ethresear.ch/t/schelling-coin-bribery-bidding-wars/7484)). The "votes" are economically real payments.

Two qualifications keep this claim honest. First, payment amounts are on-chain, but the *value* they express in physical-coin terms is an off-chain fact. Critics will call Zen an **endogenous oracle** rather than "no oracle," and the spec should accept that label. Second, decentralized measurement inherits the Sybil problem. Douceur showed that without a certifying authority, Sybil attacks are always possible except under unrealistic resource-parity assumptions ([Douceur 2002](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-sybil-attack/)). Fabricated activity is the norm where it is cheap and rewarded: wash trading averaged **over 70% of reported volume** on unregulated crypto exchanges ([Cong et al., NBER w30783](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30783/w30783.pdf)). Zen does not escape Douceur. It prices the attack, and the spec must give that price.

Several neighbouring open problems are worth naming only as secondary framing. The **stablecoin trilemma** (decentralization, stability, capital efficiency) is a widely used industry heuristic, not a theorem, with no traceable origin ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/gb/resources/more/the-stablecoin-trilemma)). Zen sidesteps capital efficiency by holding no on-chain collateral, but what it gives up is enforced convertibility. The BIS **singleness** test says private bearer tokens "typically fail" to settle at par ([BIS AER 2025 ch. III](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2025e3.pdf)). "One electronic coin trades as S grams" is a singleness claim across two forms of metal money, made without any settlement asset. The **Hayek/Graham/Keynes/Kaldor** tradition sought a commodity-anchored private or international unit, but every version needed an issuer or buffer-stock agency, and none was adopted ([Ussher, INET](https://ineteconomics.org/research/research-papers/combining-international-monetary-reform-with-commodity-buffer-stocks-keynes-graham-and-kaldor); [Hayek 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Denationalisation_of_Money)). The **Triffin dilemma** is rhetorical here: a non-national metal unit avoids the "issuer must run deficits" mechanism ([RTI Manifesto 2025](https://www.triffininternational.eu/images/2025/RTI-MANIFESTO_March2025.pdf)).

| Open problem | Status in literature | What Zen can honestly claim | What Zen cannot claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oracle problem | Open ([Klages-Mundt et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.12388v2)) | Reporter-free, consensus-computed metal reference | That the off-chain value fact becomes verifiable on-chain |
| Sybil-resistant measurement | Impossible without cost assumptions ([Douceur](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-sybil-attack/)) | A quantified cost of corruption, paid in capped coin-days and bonds | Immunity to wash payments |
| Unit-of-account failure of crypto | Documented ([Yermack](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19747)) | A unit that tracks a physical metal weight | Stable purchasing power over goods |
| Singleness / par | Fails for stablecoins ([BIS 2025](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2025e3.pdf)) | A published, consensus-wide gram rate | Par without any arbitrage or settlement channel |
| Reflexive death spirals | Terra, Iron, Basis ([Liu et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31160)) | No endogenous share token defends the peg | Immunity to confidence runs |

> **Suggested spec text: Problem statement.** Bitcoin showed that a peer-to-peer network can prevent double-spending without a trusted third party, but it left open how to give such money a stable unit of account. Every existing attempt either trusts a custodian holding the reference asset or trusts an oracle reporting its price. Zen addresses the oracle problem for a commodity-denominated unit: it derives each chain's coin weight S, in grams of a physical metal, from the chain's own payments and coin-priced shop prices, computed by every node under the same consensus rules. No party reports a price and no party holds the metal. Corrupting S requires an attacker to supply at least [X]% of the capped, coin-days-weighted observations in the trimmed window, for at least [Y] consecutive days, at a cost of at least [Z] per gram of shift (section [N]). **Caveats:** Zen does not make off-chain value verifiable on-chain. It replaces a trusted reporter with an economically costly, statistically robust estimate. S tracks metal, not a basket of goods, so purchasing power remains exposed to metal-market moves. Par between electronic and physical coins is maintained by shops' pricing and by arbitrage, not guaranteed by redemption.

## History backs the metals but not the self-stabilization claim

The designer's thesis has three parts. Physical metal coins are the best store of value. Electronic coins are the best transaction tool and an elastic "surplus" layer over them. And the three-metal, three-layer system can temper inflation and deflation without a central bank. Each part has supporting precedent, but the third is where the strongest criticisms land.

### Gresham's law binds only if metal ratios are fixed

Fixed legal ratios between metals broke down every time the market ratio moved. Under the 1792 US 15:1 mint ratio, silver drove out gold. The 1834 reset to 16:1 reversed the flow, leaving the US "legally bimetallic but de facto on gold" ([McCulloch lecture notes](https://www.asc.ohio-state.edu/mcculloch.2/econ520/lecture2.pdf)). The Latin Monetary Union's 15.5:1 ratio collapsed after silver fell around 1873. Free silver conversion was limited in 1874 and suspended by 1878, and members' debased coins spread to other members ([Wikipedia: LMU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Monetary_Union)). Silver lost **about 55% of its value against gold after 1873** ([Velde, Chicago Fed WP 2002-29](https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/working-papers/2002/wp2002-29-pdf.pdf)). Zen's answer is already in its design: Yen, Ken and Jen each float their own S, so no legal ratio exists for Gresham's law to exploit. This is a **parallel standard**, not trimetallism. The price is three units of account with fluctuating cross-rates. The spec must also state explicitly that no protocol rule, and no shop convention it encourages, fixes the cross-ratios, because the batch-auction cross-check could otherwise be read as one.

### The small-change problem was solved by fiduciary tokens, not full-bodied coins

Western Europe "long struggled to sustain a proper mix of large and small denomination coins." Full-bodied small coins cost relatively more to mint, so mints under-produced them and shortages recurred for centuries. Copper experiments collapsed whenever issue was unlimited or counterfeiting easy: Castile's vellón, Germany's Kipper- und Wipperzeit, Russia's copper rubles ([Sargent & Velde, Chicago Fed WP 97-13](https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/working-papers/1997/wp97-13-pdf.pdf)). The eventual "standard formula" made small coins **tokens**: worth less as metal than at face value, limited in quantity, and convertible by a monopoly issuer. England adopted it in 1816 and the US only in 1853 ([Sargent & Velde](https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/working-papers/1997/wp97-13-pdf.pdf)). A 2 g copper Jen worth its metal value has a tiny face value and sits exactly in the medieval trap: high minting cost relative to value, chronic under-supply, and an incentive to melt when copper rises. Zen's honest reply is that the **electronic** Jen is the small-change solution. It has no minting cost, divides into sixths, and settles over channels. But Sargent and Velde's formula worked because an issuer guaranteed convertibility. Zen must show that the electronic tier stays usable for small change when physical copper coins are scarce, and that the measurement of S on the Jen chain does not depend on a thin physical-copper market.

### Metal anchors stabilize prices over decades, not years

The gold standard "is long associated with long-run price stability," but critics at the time pointed to its short-run price variability ([Bordo, Dittmar & Gavin, NBER w10171](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10171)). Prices fell about **1.7% a year from 1873 to 1896**, then rose about 2.0% a year to 1913 ([Velde 2002](https://www.chicagofed.org/~/media/publications/working-papers/2002/wp2002-29-pdf.pdf)). Hume's "automatic" price-specie flow was "a painfully slow process," and central-bank rate policy displaced it in practice ([Wikipedia: Price–specie flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price%E2%80%93specie_flow_mechanism)). Eichengreen argues that gold was "the binding constraint" that turned the 1929 shock into a global depression, and that recovery came "only after abandoning the gold standard" ([NBER: Golden Fetters](https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/golden-fetters-gold-standard-and-great-depression-1919-1939)). The classical system was also not decentralized. It ran on central-bank discount rates and international cooperation. Zen's physical layer inherits these properties. Copper, an industrial metal, adds procyclicality: copper money would gain value in booms and lose it in slumps. That is reasoning from the metal's industrial role; no copper-versus-CPI volatility data was gathered.

### Elasticity needs a rule and a balance sheet

This is the load-bearing criticism. The BIS states it directly: a protocol-fixed supply "prevents it from being supplied elastically," so "any fluctuation in demand translates into changes in valuation." Stabilizing value requires "a central authority … which can expand or contract its balance sheet," and the instability "is unlikely to be fully overcome by better protocols or financial engineering" ([BIS AER 2018 ch. V](https://bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2018e5.htm)). The 2025 report adds that stablecoins fail the elasticity test because "the issuer's balance sheet cannot be expanded at will" ([BIS AER 2025](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2025e3.pdf)).

The free-banking literature offers the best steelman. Competing banks issuing convertible notes over a fixed specie base can expand the medium of exchange when money demand rises, keeping nominal spending stable. Selgin writes that "a banking system that promotes deflation disrupts economic activity just as surely as one that promotes inflation" ([quoted in Bagus, Mises Daily](https://mises.org/library/whos-afraid-deflation)). But that elasticity is safe only because issuers face redemption, adverse clearings and loss of capital. Zen's "elastic surplus" is therefore one of two things. (a) It is fully tied to physical stock, in which case it is not elastic and Zen inherits pure-metal deflation dynamics. (b) It is issued beyond the physical stock, in which case some rule decides how much, and that rule *is* monetary policy, whether run by a central bank, banks or code.

There is a third reading, which Zen's actual mechanism suggests and which the spec should state outright. If S floats with measured value, then a rise in demand for electronic coins raises each coin's gram weight rather than its quantity. That is **valuation elasticity**: the electronic coin absorbs demand shocks in its price in grams, while prices quoted in grams stay put. It is a coherent design, but then the electronic coin is not a stable store of value. It is a floating-rate transaction token with a trustworthy gram quote, and "the system tempers inflation" reduces to "grams of metal are a decent unit of account."

### Fisher's compensated dollar is the closest precedent, and the most flattering

Irving Fisher's plan treated the metal weight of the monetary unit as a policy variable. Each month an official price index was checked, and the dollar's gold weight rose 1% for every 1% the index stood above par. A **1% brassage** (buy/sell spread) capped each shift to block speculation ([Fisher, QJE 1914](https://people.wku.edu/charles.smith/wallace/zFisherI1914.pdf); [Stabilizing the Dollar, FRASER](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/books/stabilizing_fisher.pdf)). A modern DSGE comparison finds the compensated dollar cuts price-level and inflation uncertainty "**by an order of magnitude at all horizons**" relative to both the gold standard and inflation targeting ([Bordo, Dittmar & Gavin](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10171)). Zen's floating S over fixed-weight physical coins is structurally a decentralized compensated dollar. Fisher's "virtual dollar" was the variable bullion weight that certificates redeemed for, and he **withdrew circulating gold coins** precisely because a stamped coin cannot float ([Fisher 1914](https://people.wku.edu/charles.smith/wallace/zFisherI1914.pdf)). Zen keeps the stamped coins and floats the electronic one, which is exactly the right split.

Three differences decide whether the precedent transfers. Fisher stabilized against a **broad goods index**, while Zen measures against **metal**, so Zen gets the measurement machinery without the stabilization benefit unless it targets goods prices. Fisher's adjustment ran through a mint that bought and sold bullion at posted prices; Zen has no such arbitrage counterparty. And Fisher's brassage is the direct ancestor of Zen's anchor and band, a rate limit sized to make speculation unprofitable. Hayek's 1943 commodity-reserve proposal similarly relied on an authority buying the basket when below target and selling when above, which made it naturally countercyclical ([AIER on Hayek](https://aier.org/article/hayeks-commodity-reserve-currency/)).

### What the design must answer

To make the stabilization claim defensible, the spec needs five explicit answers. The **target**: metal value (descriptive S) or a goods index (Fisher-style). The **instrument**: floating S only, or issuance and burning of electronic coins. The **data source** for any goods reference. The **spread or band** that prevents profitable front-running, as Fisher's brassage did. And the **run scenario**: what happens when holders seek physical coins en masse and no one is obliged to supply them. Multi-member systems also invite debasement free-riding, as with the Papal States and Greece in the LMU ([Wikipedia: LMU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Monetary_Union)). Zen's analogue is any actor able to issue electronic coin cheaply against the shared gram reference.

> **Suggested spec text: Economic rationale.** Zen separates three roles that metal money has historically combined. Physical coins of fixed weight (Yen 4 g gold, Ken 3 g silver, Jen 2 g copper) serve as a store of value held privately. Electronic coins serve as the transaction medium. Each chain's consensus-measured weight S expresses the electronic coin's value in grams of its metal. Because the three chains float independently, no legal ratio between metals exists, and Gresham's law, which defeated fixed-ratio bimetallism in the United States (1792–1873) and the Latin Monetary Union (1865–1878), has no fixed ratio to exploit. Because the electronic tier divides into sixths and settles off-chain, it supplies small change without the minting cost that made full-bodied small coins chronically scarce. Zen's floating S follows the logic of Irving Fisher's compensated dollar, keeping the physical coins fixed while varying the weight of the unit of account, with a band that limits each adjustment as Fisher's brassage did. **Caveats:** Zen measures value in metal; it does not stabilize the price of goods. Metal-anchored systems have historically delivered long-run price stability alongside multi-decade deflations and severe short-run variability, and Zen's physical layer inherits this. Every historical commodity system that actively stabilized prices relied on an agent with a balance sheet (a mint, banks or a buffer-stock authority). Zen has no such agent, so any "elasticity" comes from S floating in value, not from supply expanding to meet demand. Zen does not claim to prevent inflation or deflation cycles; it claims to give every participant a common, manipulation-resistant gram reference without a central bank.

## Algorithmic money failed through reflexivity, and medians fail under sustained shifts

The record of self-regulating crypto money is brutal and instructive. Terra collapsed **within three days in May 2022, erasing about $50bn**, in a run centred on Anchor's high UST yields. The run was unlikely to have been caused by a single manipulator, and blockchain transparency let investors watch each other, which accelerated it ([Liu, Makarov & Schoar, NBER w31160](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31160)). Anchor lost about 9.5B UST (about 69% of deposits) in five days, and LUNA fell from about $87 to under $0.00005 ([Briola et al., arXiv 2207.13914](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.13914.pdf)). Iron Finance died when its TWAP oracle kept reporting TITAN above market during a crash, so redemptions minted ever more TITAN in a death spiral ([Finematics](https://finematics.com/bank-run-in-defi-iron-finance-explained/)). Model checking of Basis Cash found counterexamples in which both expansion and contraction fail. Market data matched: contraction broke from January 2021 because holders would not burn coins for bonds they feared they could not redeem ([Zhao, Li & Yuan, arXiv 2101.08423](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2101.08423)). The original Basis shut down before launch because its bond and share tokens would be securities ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/12/13/basis-backed-with-133-million-from-top-vcs-to-build-a-price-stable-cryptocurrency-says-its-shutting-down-and-returning-the-money)). NuBits held its peg for about 20 months before one large seller broke it in 2016 ([Moneyness](https://www.moneyness.ca/2016/08/end-of-stablecoin.html)). The survivors retreated to full collateral and hard redemption. Frax voted to move to 100% collateral in February 2023, saying "the costs of being slightly undercollateralized now far outweigh the benefits" ([Blockworks](https://blockworks.com/news/frax-ditches-algorithm-backing)). Liquity holds parity through a redemption floor and a 110% collateral ceiling, with parity acting as a Schelling point rather than a guarantee ([Liquity](https://www.liquity.org/blog/on-price-stability-of-liquity)).

Zen avoids the core reflexive loop because it does not defend a price by minting an endogenous share token. Fu et al. find that seigniorage-share designs are unlikely to meet rational-Ponzi sustainability conditions, while rebase designs can, as long as an active market exists ([arXiv 2210.11928](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.11928)). Three lessons still transfer. Iron's lagged TWAP warns that a day-window measurement diverging from a crashing market creates a one-sided arbitrage. Basis Cash warns against any mechanism that depends on honest users volunteering to transact under stress. Terra warns that a publicly computable S lets everyone anticipate the next update, so updates must not create predictable, profitable jumps.

On control design, RAI is the canonical on-chain feedback controller, and in practice it ran **proportional-only**: "Reflexer's controller uses only the left-most (proportional) term" ([Strohmeyer et al., arXiv 2410.21446](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21446v1)). Ampleforth gates rebases on a 5% deviation and caps them with a sigmoid ([MixBytes](https://www.mixbytes.io/blog/security-of-algorithmic-stablecoins)). Zen's anchor and band act like a slew-rate limit. That trades responsiveness for manipulation resistance, because an attacker must sustain a distortion for many periods. An integral term would be risky, since undetected wash flow would compound. Zen is best positioned as a non-discretionary quantity rule in the Friedman tradition. Kiayias et al. find the Friedman rule optimal for token supply in steady state ([arXiv 2306.17025](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.17025)).

Manipulation-resistant measurement draws on four bodies of evidence. **Coin age** is Peercoin's primitive: "currency amount times holding period," consumed when spent ([King & Nadal 2012](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/papers-we-love/papers-we-love/refs/heads/main/digital_currency/peercoin.pdf)). Cycling the same coins A→B→A quickly destroys almost no coin-days, so high-frequency self-payment adds little weight. The known weakness is that an attacker can let large balances age quietly and then spend them all at once, which Zen's receiver caps, trimming and band must absorb. Coin age is also only as trustworthy as its timestamps, so rest periods should be measured in block heights. **Robust aggregation**: a time-weighted median forces an attacker to control more than half the window ([Uniswap Labs](https://blog.uniswap.org/uniswap-v3-oracles)). A middle-20% trimmed mean discards 40% of observations from each tail, so its breakdown point is 40% per side. But robustness to outliers is not robustness to a sustained shift, and the ethresear.ch debate on medians versus TWAPs remains unresolved ([ethresear.ch](https://ethresear.ch/t/median-prices-as-alternative-to-twap-an-optimised-proof-of-concept-analysis-and-simulation/12778)). **Block-producer power**: after the Merge, moving a USDC/WETH TWAP 20% costs about $709B in a 2-block attack but only about **$978M in a 3-block attack**. A validator with 10% stake gets three consecutive blocks about 181 times a month ([Uniswap Labs](https://blog.uniswap.org/uniswap-v3-oracles)). Zen's producers can both create and include wash payments, so producer-originated payments need discounting. **Thin cross-markets**: Mango Markets' oracle price rose more than 13x in about 30 minutes when one trader moved the thin external markets it read, and over $110M was withdrawn ([DWT on the CFTC action](https://dwt.com/blogs/financial-services-law-advisor/2023/01/cftc-decentralized-manipulation-crypto)). Zen's median-of-three batch-auction cross-check needs a minimum-liquidity condition, or it becomes a Mango-style attack surface. Flash-loan attacks on bZx returned over 500,000% by borrowing unbounded capital atomically ([Qin et al., arXiv 2003.03810](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03810v1)). Zen's coin-days weighting is a natural defence against them, since freshly borrowed coins carry no age.

> **Suggested spec text: Measurement security.** Zen's coin weight S is computed deterministically by every node. It uses no reported votes, so the bribery attacks on stake-weighted Schelling-point oracles do not apply. The attack surface is instead (a) payments created to distort the measurement and (b) inclusion and ordering by block producers. Zen defends against (a) by counting each gram once per rest period, measured in block heights, and by weighting observations by value and coin-days destroyed, so rapidly cycled or freshly borrowed coins carry little weight. Receiver caps bound any single receiver's influence, and bonds grow while a receiver's reports disagree with the consensus estimate. Observations are aggregated with a trimmed estimator whose breakdown point is [40]% of capped, coin-days-weighted observations on one side. Zen defends against (b) by [discounting producer-originated payments]. The published S may move at most [b]% per day from its anchor, so a distortion must be sustained for at least [n] days to move S by [x]%. The median-of-three cross-check through the on-chain batch auction counts only auctions that clear at least [L] grams of volume. When the payment-based estimate and the cross-check diverge by more than [d]%, S [freezes / widens its band]. **Caveats:** no estimator is immune to a sufficiently resourced attacker who controls a large share of real payment volume for a sustained period. The band that resists manipulation also delays legitimate repricing, as lagged TWAPs did in Iron Finance's collapse. Coin-days weighting can be gamed by patiently aged balances. The bounds above are design targets that require simulation and adversarial testing before launch.

## Channel factories exist, but unanimity is still the binding constraint

Lightning shows both the promise and the limits of two-party channels. Public capacity has stayed roughly flat at **about 4,900–5,600 BTC across about 41,000–43,000 public channels** through 2025–2026 ([Spark, State of Lightning 2026](https://www.spark.money/research/lightning-network-2026-state); the figures are relayed from mempool.space by a competing L2, so treat the framing with care). The persistent pain points are inbound liquidity, force closes that lock funds for days or weeks, and HTLC attacks such as replacement cycling (disclosed in October 2023). All of them stem from one channel per on-chain output ([Spark 2026](https://www.spark.money/research/lightning-network-2026-state)).

Channel factories are the canonical fix. Burchert, Decker and Wattenhofer put many channels under one n-of-n funding output. For 20 users and 100 channels this cuts on-chain cost by **90%**, or an estimated **96%** with Schnorr aggregation ([Royal Society Open Science 2018](https://doaj.org/article/39e059f7493041ed8b6bb6e47c5887a4); [PDF](https://tik-old.ee.ethz.ch/file/a20a865ce40d40c8f942cf206a7cba96/Scalable_Funding_Of_Blockchain_Micropayment_Networks.pdf)). The structure has three layers: an on-chain n-of-n funding transaction, off-chain allocation transactions that need all n signers, and ordinary two-party channels. As of mid-2026, **no production Lightning implementation supports factories** ([Spark, Channel factories](https://www.spark.money/research/channel-factories-scaling-lightning)). ZmnSCPxj's SuperScalar (2024) arranges Decker-Wattenhofer factories as laddered timeout trees with LSP-to-user channels at the leaves, and needs no consensus change ([Bitcoin Optech, 2024-11-01](https://bitcoinops.org/en/newsletters/2024/11/01/); [Delving Bitcoin](https://delvingbitcoin.org/t/superscalar-laddered-timeout-tree-structured-decker-wattenhofer-factories/1143/)). A July 2026 implementation report describes one MuSig2 output shared by an LSP and **up to 127 clients**. It demonstrated a 127-client cooperative close in a single signet transaction and a unilateral exit at 1 sat/vB. It has no external audit, its splicing is a stub, and it notes that a CTV covenant would remove the signing ceremonies and signer limit ([Delving Bitcoin implementation report](https://delvingbitcoin.org/t/superscalar-an-implementation-report/2705)). Ark is the adjacent shared-UTXO design and is live on Bitcoin mainnet through Second (Sept 2024) ([Second](https://second.tech/blog/ark-on-bitcoin-is-here)) and Arkade (public beta Oct 2025, VTXOs with unilateral exit) ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/375271/ark-labs-arkade-public-beta-layer-2-bitcoin)).

The constraint shared by every one of these designs is **unanimity**. Every allocation update needs all n signatures, so one offline participant blocks updates, and without covenants the choice is all-cooperate or all-on-chain ([Spark, Channel factories](https://www.spark.money/research/channel-factories-scaling-lightning)). Bitcoin's covenant path remains uncertain. A CTV activation client was released in February 2026, but miner signaling was 0% in May 2026. CSFS (BIP 348) is merged, OP_CAT (BIP 347) is marked Complete, and OP_TEMPLATEHASH (BIP 446) is a Draft running on Inquisition signet ([Spark, Covenant activation](https://www.spark.money/research/bitcoin-covenant-activation-path-forward); [BIP 446](https://www.spark.money/glossary/bip-446)). LN-Symmetry (eltoo) exists only as a signet proof of concept. Its recap reports that **pinning resistance is most of the work**, that worst-case disputes take about twice the update timelock, and that the design generalizes more naturally to multi-party channels ([LN-Symmetry recap](https://delvingbitcoin.org/t/ln-symmetry-project-recap/359); [eltoo paper](https://blockstream.com/eltoo.pdf)). Zen controls its own consensus. It can therefore ship a template covenant, rebindable (eltoo-style) state updates, package relay and fee sponsorship from day one. Those are exactly the primitives Bitcoin designs work around.

Channels compress usage but never remove dependence on the chain. Opens, closes, splices, factory exits and disputes all need block space within timelock windows, and the original Lightning paper names "forced expiration spam" as a systemic risk ([Lightning paper](https://lightning.network/lightning-network-paper.pdf)). For streams, one-way Spilman-style channels avoid revocation state, as SuperScalar's pseudo-Spilman leaves show. A monotone-balance, expiry-bounded stream primitive is cheaper than a duplex channel. That is an inference; no streaming-payment sources were gathered. The canonical design-space references are the layer-two SoKs ([Gudgeon et al., ePrint 2019/360](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/360); [Jourenko et al., ePrint 2019/352](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/352)).

Channels also interact with Zen's measurement in a way Bitcoin never faced. Off-chain payments do not appear on-chain, so S is measured only from on-chain payments, channel settlements and auction trades. As scaling succeeds, the observable sample shrinks and skews toward large and settlement flows. The spec must either define how channel closes contribute observations or accept that S will be measured from a less representative sample.

> **Suggested spec text: Scaling layer.** Zen scales through four primitives built into its base protocol: on-chain payments; unidirectional stream channels, in which the payer signs monotonically increasing balances and which carry no revocation state; bidirectional channels with symmetric, rebindable state updates; and channel factories, in which many channels share one n-of-n funding output and allocations are enforced by a native template covenant. This follows the channel-factory construction of Burchert, Decker and Wattenhofer (2018), which reduced on-chain cost by about 90% for 20 users and 100 channels, and the laddered timeout-tree design of SuperScalar. Because Zen's covenant is native, a single participant can exit a factory without forcing the full state on-chain. Factory expiries are staggered so that mass exits do not compete for the same blocks. The minimum dispute window exceeds the worst-case time to confirm a mass exit at the block-size limit, and [a dispute lane / fee sponsorship] protects time-critical transactions from pinning. **Caveats:** off-chain designs reduce, but do not eliminate, on-chain load; mass-exit events remain the binding capacity constraint. Factories still require coordination among participants for cooperative updates. Payments that stay off-chain do not contribute to the measurement of S; section [X] specifies which channel events count as observations.

## Conclusion

The research changes where Zen's argument should rest. Zen's strongest contribution is not monetary stability. It is a **measurement primitive**: a reporter-free, in-consensus, Sybil-priced estimate of a coin's value in grams of metal. That is a concrete answer to an acknowledged open problem, and it can be stated with a breakdown point and an attack cost in the way Nakamoto stated his catch-up probability. The macro thesis is better reframed as "Fisher's compensated dollar without a mint." The stamped physical coins stay fixed, the electronic unit floats, and the anchor and band play the role of brassage. That framing is historically grounded and flattering, and it also states plainly what is missing: a goods-price target and an arbitrage counterparty. Without those, the honest claim is a common, uncensorable metal reference, not tempered business cycles.

Three items decide whether reviewers take Zen seriously. First, the spec must name where the physical-metal anchor enters, so the measurement cannot become self-referential once shops price by S. Second, it must give simulated cost-of-corruption numbers for the trimmed, coin-days-weighted, capped estimator under producer collusion and patient coin aging. Third, it must reconcile scaling with measurement, because every payment moved into a channel is a payment the peg no longer sees. A Zen that states these limits will be more credible than one that claims to have solved stable money.
